The short answer

The scores measure fear accurately, but fear is a poor timing signal. When the panic score is high, the next month really is more volatile and deeper drops really are more likely. What the score does not do is tell you when to buy. Higher fear has been followed by slightly better returns on average, but the effect is tiny: the most-feared tenth of stocks beat the least-feared tenth by 0.6% over the next month, and the score explains roughly 0.2% of what happens next. It has also faded to nothing since 2020, and it reverses at one year.

The “exhaustion” half of both models has no edge. On the index, higher exhaustion readings were followed by lower returns, and the ladder’s top rung never fired for SPY in 33 years. On single stocks, “stabilizing” is the label on 49% of all days, and “falling knife” days did better afterwards than “stabilizing” days.

Context matters more than the score. A stock that panics while the market is calm has kept underperforming. A stock that panics in a market-wide selloff has rebounded with the market. The dashboard does not distinguish the two.

Index capitulation ladder

The risk page’s ladder runs the intraday forced-flow model on daily bars for SPY, QQQ, IWM, the sector SPDRs and macro ETFs. It was replayed every session with the production code, including the two-session hysteresis and the drawdown gate. The replay matched all 31 rows the dashboard has published since 10 August.

The ladder spent 5.7% of sessions at “stress” or above. The upper rungs are the problem. On daily bars, the exhaustion confirmations (an up day, a close in the upper half) flip from one session to the next, so the required two-session dwell almost never holds. SPY’s raw model said “confirmed exhaustion” on 15 days, including 26 Dec 2018 and 13 Oct 2022, each the session after a major low. It also said it on 7 Jul 2008 and 2 Mar 2020, with much further to fall. None of the 15 was ever published.

The one rung that carries information is stress: SPY’s next 63 sessions averaged +5.9% against +2.9% on all days. Compared only with days at the same drawdown that didn’t fire, it still added +2.7%. It is no better than simple rules, though: a 5% five-day drop or a VIX close above 30 did as well or better. Over the full S&P 500 history from 1962 its edge shrinks to +0.7% (t +0.6). Buying stress worked unusually well after 1993.

Per-stock fear scores

Every dashboard stock with price history was replayed every session from its first trade, using today’s production model: up to 64 years for the oldest names. Scores are only used once a stock has the full three-year percentile window the model expects. Outcomes are log returns from the next session’s close, minus SPY’s (or the local index’s abroad), so a market-wide rebound doesn’t count as stock-specific skill.

The pattern is a slope in risk, not in return. Moving from calm to extreme readings, the chance of another 15% drop goes from 15% to 32% and next-month volatility from 24% to 60%. Returns relative to SPY move by about a percentage point across the whole range, and only at the very top.

Split by what the market was doing, the result changes. Stock-specific panics, with the market calm, were followed by flat-to-negative relative returns. That is consistent with the well-documented drift after company-specific bad news. Panics during market-wide selloffs were followed by positive relative returns, and much larger absolute ones, as high-beta names bounced with the market. Part of that is beta rather than skill.

Ranked against each other on the same day, higher-panic stocks beat lower-panic stocks by +0.62% over 21 sessions (top vs bottom decile, t +5.4). About half of that is plain short-term reversal: controlling for the last 5 and 20 days’ return, momentum, volatility and drawdown cuts the panic coefficient from +0.032 to +0.018 (t +5.7). Over a full year the ranking reverses: the top decile trailed the bottom by 1.4%, the momentum effect. The exhaustion score is negative here too (IC −0.014), because it mostly records whether the stock just had an up day. All figures are before trading costs, which would absorb most of a 21-session spread this size.

Problems found along the way

  1. “Stabilizing after stress” has no stress gate. The stock setup calls any day with exhaustion ≥ 40 “stabilizing”, including calm up-days at all-time highs. The index ladder already fixed this with its exhaustion-meaningful gate; the stock model never got it.
  2. The ladder’s top rungs can’t fire on daily bars. The two-session dwell was set for minute bars. On daily bars it has suppressed every confirmed-exhaustion reading for SPY since 1993.
  3. Two market-fear reads disagree. The risk page ladder says “normal”; the holdings page’s SPY read says “stress building” (pressure 79). They are different models with different thresholds, and nothing on the page says so.
  4. DNP.WA shows technicals for the wrong company. The Yahoo symbol resolves to DNP, the DNP Select Income Fund on NYSE, not Dino Polska in Warsaw (DNP.WA).
  5. Delisted names vanish. Yahoo returns a single bar for AVB, EA, EQR, LBRDK and TOI. Their dashboard rows are preserved snapshots. The same mechanism quietly removes failures from any history-based study.
  6. Inherited histories and corrupt adjustments. Some tickers carry a predecessor company’s history (WULF before 2021 is IKONICS). 28 histories contain one-day moves above 3×, several of them reverse-split artifacts (XTIA’s adjusted price reaches 2×1013). This study drops the year either side of such moves.
  7. The cached SPY benchmark is a bot-check page. _system/reference/market-data/benchmarks/spy_us.csv contains Stooq’s JavaScript challenge HTML, not prices.

The study plan

Phases 1 to 4 were run for this report. Phases 5 to 7 are what it would take to make the conclusions decision-grade and keep them current.

  1. Pre-register the questions

    Write down what each label claims before looking at outcomes. Fix three primary endpoints:

    • SPY’s 63-session return after the ladder reaches “stress”, against all days and against days at the same drawdown.
    • The daily cross-sectional rank correlation of panic with 21- and 63-session excess returns.
    • Each stock’s panic-to-63-session correlation against its own time-shifted null.

    Everything else is exploratory and gets a false-discovery correction.

    Done for this pass

  2. Assemble the longest clean history

    Full daily OHLCV for every dashboard stock and the 23 ladder ETFs, from the same source and adjustment as production. Screen for:

    • Wrong-security mappings.
    • Delisted symbols.
    • Inherited histories.
    • Impossible one-day moves.

    Done · 680 stocks with 3+ usable years, 4.9 million stock-days

  3. Replay the signals point-in-time

    Recompute every score as the nightly job would have published it that evening, using only data up to that close. Prove the replay against the production code, not against itself.

    Done · exact match on 560 random snapshots

  4. Score accuracy and precision

    Accuracy asks whether the signal points the right way: rank correlation, hit rate against the base rate, AUC, and decile slopes. Precision has three senses:

    • How often a firing is followed by the outcome it implies.
    • How tight those outcomes are.
    • How close to the actual low it fires.

    Run each test across horizons of 1 to 252 sessions, both as risk (further drawdown, volatility) and as return. Compare against simple rules and standard factors.

    Done · this report

  5. Remove survivorship and beta

    Replicate the stock results on a point-in-time universe that includes delisted companies. That requires a vendor with dead-company history (CRSP, Norgate or Sharadar); Yahoo drops them. Measure excess returns against a beta-matched benchmark, and net out realistic trading costs.

    Next · needs a licensed data source

  6. Track every published signal forward

    Log each nightly score and ladder state as it is published, and grade it when its 21-, 63- and 252-session outcomes mature. This is the only true out-of-sample test. It also catches drift between the live job and this replay, such as missed runs, data revisions or code changes. Show the running record on the dashboard beside the signal.

    Next · small nightly job, no new data needed

  7. Recalibrate, with a holdout

    Only after phases 5–6:

    • Gate “stabilizing” on real stress.
    • Shorten or drop the daily-bar dwell for the exhaustion rungs.
    • Show market context next to each stock’s panic.
    • Re-test any change walk-forward on a held-out period before it reaches the page.

    Later · decision rules below

Decision rules

  • If a label’s historical outcomes don’t differ from the baseline at the pre-registered horizon, relabel it as description (“high volatility”, “sharp decline”) rather than advice (“falling knife”, “stabilizing”).
  • Keep panic and pressure as risk gauges: they reliably precede higher volatility. Show the stock’s own realized volatility next to them, since it predicts risk better.
  • Treat the index “stress” rung as a regime flag, not a buy signal, until the 1962–1992 weakness is explained.
  • Retire or rebuild the exhaustion rungs. Ten years of forward tracking with no edge is enough to remove them from the page.

Method and caveats

  • Replay. Index ladder: production flow_stress.calculate_flow_snapshot, apply_state_hysteresis and build_capitulation_daily gates, run once per session on a 100-bar window. That window was checked identical to the production 2-year call on 120 samples. Stock model: a vectorized replay of build_technical_signals.calculate_snapshot, matched against the production function on 560 random (stock, date) snapshots. Every state, phase and confirmation was identical; the only differences were 0.1–1.0 percentile points in four tie-heavy 1990s penny-stock histories.
  • Data. Yahoo chart v8 daily bars with the production dividend adjustment. Before SPY existed (Jan 1993) the stock benchmark is the S&P 500 price index, so relative-strength inputs match production exactly from mid-1996.
  • Outcomes. Entry at the next session’s close, because the dashboard updates overnight. Next-open and same-close entries were also computed and change nothing material. Stock averages use log returns to keep a few multi-baggers and bad prints from dominating.
  • Inference. Newey-West errors with lags equal to the horizon for overlapping windows; bootstrap by calendar month for events, because a crash month produces hundreds of simultaneous signals that are really one observation; circular-shift nulls and Benjamini-Hochberg for per-stock tests.
  • Survivorship. The universe is today’s dashboard, so companies that failed are missing. That flatters “buy the fear” results. The positive stock-level effects here are upper bounds, and the index results, which have no survivorship problem, are the cleaner test.
  • Selection. Many names were added to the dashboard after a large move. Pre-2000 data for small and foreign listings is thinner and noisier. Excess returns are measured against SPY, not beta-adjusted.
