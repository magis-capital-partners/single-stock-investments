## What this business is

Fair Isaac sells a credit score and the software that uses scores in lending decisions. The three national credit bureaus, Experian, TransUnion, and Equifax, run the formula on their files and pay Fair Isaac a fee for each score. A smaller direct-to-consumer business sells scores at myFICO.com. The software segment covers fraud, origination, account management, and a subscription platform. In the year ended September 30, 2025, Scores were $1.17 billion and Software was $822 million.

## Why the market might be wrong

The September 29 selloff prices a break in mortgage score pricing power. That reading fits the last quarterly report, which said business-to-business score growth came mainly from a higher mortgage unit price. It does not fit a claim that the whole company just lost its customers. Classic FICO is still allowed at Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, and it is still required when a loan is underwritten by hand. The market can be wrong in either direction: too fearful if lenders keep paying up for the familiar score, or not fearful enough if the bureaus, who own the cheaper rival and distribute FICO, steer volume away.

## Executive summary

On September 29, 2026, Fair Isaac closed at $617.87, down 26.5% on the day and 51% below the July 20 close in the valuation file. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac will price Classic FICO and VantageScore 4.0 on one mortgage grid, and they dropped a 20-point penalty on the rival score. Rocket Mortgage said it will prefer VantageScore. The last quarter did not show a volume collapse. Scores revenue was $459 million, up from $324 million, and management tied the business-to-business increase to a higher mortgage unit price. Long-term debt was already $5.28 billion at June 30, including a $1.5 billion term loan used to buy back stock. If free cash flow grows 4% a year for five years and 3% after that, and the business is worth 16 times year-ten cash flow, the return is about **9%** per year. That prices a toll that can no longer be raised at will. A real cut in mortgage score fees would leave a much smaller return. Stance stays watch.

## Business & moat

Fair Isaac is a toll collector on credit decisions, not a lender. The annual report describes two segments. Scores are the toll. Software is the set of tools lenders use around the decision. In fiscal 2025, business-to-business scores were $949 million, 81% of Scores, and direct-to-consumer scores were $220 million. Operating income was 88% of Scores revenue, the same as the year before. Software revenue was $822 million and grew 3%, while Software operating income fell $9.8 million. Nine months into fiscal 2026, Scores were already $1.24 billion, more than the entire prior year, and total company revenue was $1.88 billion. Quarter revenue was $674 million. Operating income for the nine months was $999 million. Interest expense, net, for those nine months was $146 million, up from $94 million. The quarterly report ties the higher interest to a higher average debt balance: the $1.5 billion of notes issued in May 2025, $1.0 billion of 6.25% notes issued in March 2026, a $1.5 billion term loan borrowed in full on June 5, 2026, and a higher balance on the revolving credit line. The June term loan matures May 15, 2028, and the company used it to fund an accelerated share repurchase. Long-term debt at June 30, 2026 was $5.28 billion, against $2.66 billion at September 30, 2025.

The distribution fact matters more after this week than it did in July. In the June quarter, agreements with the three bureaus were 63% of revenue, up from 54% a year earlier, and up from 51% for all of fiscal 2025. Those same three bureaus own VantageScore. They are the shelf and the rival.

The annual report already names the two risks that arrived as news. One is regulatory attention to mortgage closing costs, which "may affect our ability to implement price changes for FICO Scores used in mortgage originations." The other is the Federal Housing Finance Agency's process for approving score models at Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac. The September news is that process being used. Early in September, lenders delivering loans to the two mortgage giants could use Classic FICO or VantageScore 4.0, but VantageScore was marked down 20 points so it would not look stronger than it is. On September 28, Director Bill Pulte said the two grids become one, and that 20-point mark-down is gone. A borrower scored at 780 on either model can reach the same top pricing band. Classic FICO remains an option. It is still required when a human underwrites the loan. FICO's newer 10T score is not approved for delivery to Fannie and Freddie. The housing agency that insures other loans, FHA, plans to start using 10T in January 2027.

Rocket Mortgage said it will be the first lender to prefer VantageScore 4.0 on eligible loans, after running both models on 1.4 million credit reports. Borrowers who saved money saved about $1,600 at closing, on Rocket's account. United Wholesale Mortgage has said about a quarter of its borrowers score better on VantageScore, and that share might reach 40% by the end of September. Those are lender statements, not Fair Isaac's books.

What the selloff does not change. Software, myFICO, card and auto scores, and scores used to manage accounts after the loan is made are not the mortgage pricing grid. The filing does not split mortgage origination scores out of business-to-business scores, so nobody honest can say "X percent of profit just vanished." What the filing does say is that the latest jump in business-to-business scores was "primarily attributable to a higher mortgage origination scores unit price." The growth the market had been paying for is the line the policy just contested.

### Mental models

This is a standard that became a habit, then a requirement, then a price. Requirements can be rewritten. Habits change more slowly. The bureaus have a reason to hurry the change, because they keep more of a VantageScore fee than of a FICO fee. Lenders have a reason to hurry if the score lets them offer a cheaper rate. Investors who assume the unit price only goes up are using last year's mechanism.

#### Thematic context

The context file for September 29 marks a wider high-yield credit spread. That is a backdrop for mortgage volume, which is the other half of score fees. It is not an input to the return below. The event that changes the story is the pricing grid, not the spread.

Moat. The advantage on mortgage origination scores narrowed this week from "the score the mortgage giants require" to "one of two scores, and the expensive one." The advantage on everything else is intact until someone shows volume leaving. A moat that depends on a single regulator's grid is a franchise with a political lease, not a law of nature.

## Risks & inversion

**Primary risk:** Mortgage origination score prices move toward the rival's price, and the bureaus prefer the score they own. That is also the public short, including Steve Eisman's: years of mortgage score price increases gave lenders a reason to switch once the mortgage giants allowed a second score. His fee comparison is a press claim, not a line in the annual report.

Inversion: the selloff is a gift if lenders keep ordering FICO because capital markets, insurers, and the enterprises that buy the loans still want one familiar number. A second risk is leverage. Net debt is about $5.0 billion ($5.28 billion of debt minus $248 million of cash, both from the June 30 balance sheet). At a $13.3 billion equity value, that debt is a much larger slice of the enterprise than it was at $1,264 a share. The company added a large part of that debt in 2026 to retire shares, including a June term loan struck before this drop. Interest already rose. A third risk is concentration. The three bureaus were 63% of last quarter's revenue.

#### IRR arithmetic (show your work)

1. Starting free cash flow per share is $36.06.
2. After five years at 4%, free cash flow per share is $43.87.
3. After five more years at 3%, year-ten free cash flow per share is $50.86.
4. Sixteen times that cash flow is an exit value of $814 per share.
5. The buyer also receives each year's cash flow along the way, starting from $36 and ending near $51.
6. The single rate that turns a $617.87 purchase into those ten cash flows plus the $814 exit is 9% per year.

**Upside / downside from price:** The same path was about a zero return at the July 20 price of $1,263.83, and about 5% per year at the September 28 close of $840.89. The selloff did the work of lowering the price. It did not raise the cash flow. If cash flow stays at $36 per share and the exit is only 12 times, the return is about 3% per year.

**Returns statement:** About **9%** per year at the September 29 close, if cash flow grows modestly and mortgage score fees do not reset downward.
